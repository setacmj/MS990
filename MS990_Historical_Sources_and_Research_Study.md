# Wellcome MS.990: Historical Provenance, Authorship, Printed Sources, and Future Lines of Enquiry

## Executive Summary and Revision of Catalogue Metadata

Wellcome Collection MS.990 has long been catalogued as an anonymous medical and surgical compendium compiled around 1675 by a member of the Franciscan Order in Austria or Southern Germany, while the twentieth-century English morocco binding carries the gold-stamped spine date `CA 1650–1680`. A comprehensive paleographical and philological examination of the complete 741-page manuscript—cross-referenced against seventeenth-century printed medical treatises, Inner Austrian monastic records, and ecclesiastical biographies—substantially revises our understanding of the codex. Internal textual evidence establishes that the author-compiler was **Frater Bernardin**, a non-ordained lay brother (*Laienbruder*) and monastic surgeon-apothecary of the **Styrian Capuchin Province** centered in **Graz**, who compiled at least the final third of the manuscript in or after **July 1696**. Far from being an isolated provincial recipe book, MS.990 represents a sophisticated late-seventeenth-century synthesis of Renaissance vernacular ophthalmology, Paracelsian and post-Paracelsian iatrochemistry, baroque field surgery, sympathetic magnetic medicine, and charitable domestic pharmacy, all adapted and illustrated for clinical use within the Capuchin infirmary network of Inner Austria (Styria and Carinthia).

## Authorship and Monastic Provenance: Frater Bernardin of the Capuchin Friary in Graz

Although the manuscript lacks a formal title page—opening immediately on Canvas 7 (`p. 29` of the original pagination) amidst a run of remedies for head and dental ailments—the compiler reveals his name, religious status, order, and home city in a cluster of autobiographical case narratives embedded within the treatise on sympathetic medicine (Canvases 450–456, original `pp. 505–511`). On Canvas 450 (`p. 505`, right column, line 6), while recounting a theological dinner-table debate over the legitimacy of the sympathetic powder (*Pulvis Sympatheticus*), the compiler records his interlocutor's direct form of address: *"darauf sprach der Jesuwiter: mein lieber Frater Bernardin"* ("Thereupon the Jesuit spoke: my dear Brother Bernardin"). Throughout the codex, Bernardin consistently distinguishes his own rank as *Frater*—a non-ordained lay brother trained in pharmacy, bloodletting, and surgery—from the ordained priests of his community, whom he calls *Patres* (such as *"unser Pater Prediger"*, "our Father Preacher").

Two pages later, on Canvas 452 (`p. 507`, left column, lines 21–23), Frater Bernardin anchors his institutional home in **Graz**, the capital of the Duchy of Styria, while providing a decisive topographical clue to his specific religious order. Describing a severe inflammation in the hand of one of his community's preachers, he writes: *"ich befande mich eines mall zu Grätz. und in dem alten Kloster in der Stadt. eben unser Pater Prediger in dem ersten Kloster ware mit ein grausamen hand schmerz behaft..."* ("I found myself once at Graz, and in the old friary in the city; just then our Father Preacher in the first friary was afflicted with a cruel pain in the hand"). In late seventeenth-century Graz, only the **Order of Friars Minor Capuchin (OFMCap)** operated two separate houses whose relationship was defined internally as "the old friary in the city" (*das alte Kloster in der Stadt* / *das erste Kloster*) versus a newer suburban foundation. The first Capuchin friary in Graz—the *Stiegenkloster* or Antoniuskloster near the Paulustor (today the site of the Volkskundemuseum)—was founded by Archduke Ferdinand in 1600 and consecrated in 1602 within the upper city fortifications. Because the community grew rapidly, a second Graz Capuchin friary was erected in 1648–1652 outside the inner walls *"Am Graben"* (*Grabenkloster*). Only a friar belonging to the Styrian Capuchin province would naturally distinguish his residence in Graz as *"the old friary in the city, the first friary"*.

This Capuchin identity is corroborated across both the pharmaceutical text and the pictorial cycle of MS.990. On Canvas 645 (`p. 708`, left column, lines 4–6), Bernardin heads a section of travel salves with an explicitly in-house monastic formula: *"Erstlich ein Köstliches schmaltz Pflaster für die Capuciner. so sie auf Zehrung fassen ~"* ("First, a precious lard-plaster for the Capuchins, which they take along for sustenance on their journeys"), designed to treat foot blisters and leg ulcers incurred by mendicant friars traveling barefoot in sandals across the Alpine provinces. Visually, the full-page watercolor frontispiece to the ophthalmic pharmacy on Canvas 733 (original `p. 542`, *Augen-Apotheke*), as well as the apothecary interiors on Canvases 322 (`p. 352`) and 381 (`p. 417`), depicts the monastic apothecary dispensing remedies while wearing the unmistakable brown habit, long pointed hood (*Kapuze*), white knotted cord, suspended rosary, and full beard characteristic of the seventeenth-century Capuchin reform, in contrast to the clean-shaven faces and rounded cowls of the Conventual and Observant Franciscans.

## Redating the Codex (*Terminus Post Quem* of July 1696) and the Inner Austrian Elite Network

The discovery of an explicit date in the primary scribal hand on Canvas 454 (`p. 509`, right column, lines 23–29) moves the completion of MS.990 roughly two decades later than previously assumed. When Frater Bernardin first introduced his two conversational defenses of sympathetic medicine on Canvas 450 (`p. 505`), he prudently withheld the names of the noble host, the Jesuit theologian, and the bishop whom he quoted. Four pages later, however, anticipating learned readers who might dismiss anonymous anecdotes as friars' fables, he appended a rubricated `NB:` (`pp. 509–510`, Canvases 454–455) naming his witnesses, places, and dates to prove his veracity (*"wolt ihr disen Zeugen widersprechen"*, "would you contradict these witnesses?").

The first dated entry reads: *"In dem Jahr 1696. in monat Juli. dahe ich bey Ihro gnad[en] herrn Von Herberstein. Zu Laboth [or Labach/Lankowitz] auf seiner herschafft. eines tags kam 2 Jesuit[en] brüder. einer mit Namen pater Höpel [or Högel] Regens in Convicto alda u[nd] mit auch bey der taffel saß..."* ("In the year 1696, in the month of July, when I was with His Grace the Lord von Herberstein on his lordship/estate; one day two Jesuit fathers came, one by the name of Father Höpel [or Högel], Regent in the Convict there, and also sat with us at table"). Because this passage on `p. 509` is written continuously in the main text column by the same hand and in the same ink sequence that runs through the end of the surgical books (`p. 790`, Canvas 710) and the three-column alphabetical *Register* (Canvases 711–718), the entire latter third of the volume—and the final assembly and indexing of the codex—cannot have been written before **July 1696**.

Every figure named in Frater Bernardin's `NB:` belongs to the verifiable aristocratic, ecclesiastical, and academic elite of Inner Austria at the close of the seventeenth century. The **House of Herberstein** was the premier noble dynasty of Styria, owning major seats at Castle Herberstein, Eggenberg/Graz, and Lankowitz, while a Graz-born scion of the family, **Sigismund Christoph Graf von Herberstein (1644–1716)**, served as Prince-Bishop of Ljubljana (*Laibach*) from 1683 to 1716. The Jesuit interlocutor, **Pater Högel (or Höpel), S.J., *Regens in Convicto***, held the post of Regent of the Jesuit seminary boarding-college (*Convict*, such as the *Ferdinandeum* or noble *Konvikt* attached to the Jesuit University of Graz or the Jesuit College in Ljubljana). Bernardin reports that the Jesuit Regent defended the natural operation of the sympathetic powder by testifying that the Jesuits themselves prepared and used it inside their own *Collegium*, and by citing a formal moral responsum obtained from a **Professor of Theology in Paris** (*"Theolligo in Paris gefragt"*, Canvas 455, `p. 510`, left column, lines 6–15), which ruled that occult natural remedies whose efficacy cannot be proved to arise from a demonic pact may be employed with a clear Catholic conscience.

Immediately following the Herberstein episode, on Canvas 455 (`p. 510`, left column, lines 19–26, and right column, lines 1–5), Frater Bernardin names his highest-ranking patient: **Prince-Bishop Franz Kaspar von Stadion (1637–1704), Bishop of Lavant** (*"bischoff Von Lauandt. herrn Stadian. welcher biß 9 wochen bedinete"*). Franz Kaspar von Stadion had served as Imperial Diet director at Regensburg from 1666 to 1672 before ruling the Carinthian-Styrian Diocese of Lavant (seated at St. Andrä im Lavanttal) from **1673 until his death in February 1704**. Frater Bernardin notes that he personally attended Prince-Bishop Stadion as medical caregiver for up to nine weeks alongside a university-trained physician whom he names as **`docktor heibt`** (*Doctor Heibt* or *Heubt*), and remarks admiringly on the bishop's erudition and extensive diplomatic travels (*"der so hoch gelehrt. vill Comision u[nd] Raisen gehabt"*), an exact reflection of Stadion's career in imperial diplomacy prior to his episcopate.

Elsewhere in the manuscript, Bernardin's clinical horizon spans Vienna and the Eastern Alpine lands. On Canvas 9 (`p. 32`, left column), in an entry on the South American oleoresin **Balsam of Copaiba** (*Balsamo Copauia*), he observes that this balsam *"was brought some years ago out of the Indies, and therewith in Vienna great cures have been performed"* (*"ist vor etlichen Jahren auß Indien gebracht worden. vnd darmit in Wienn grosse Curen gethan worden"*), reflecting the arrival of Brazilian Copaiba in the Habsburg capital following Willem Piso and Georg Marggraf's *Historia Naturalis Brasiliae* (1648) and its adoption in Viennese court pharmacy during the 1670s and 1680s. He also preserves specialized recipes attributed to regional practitioners, including a calamine and litharge ulcer plaster from **`Doctor Hausenbach`** (Canvas 645, `p. 708`) and an empirical breast-cancer regimen credited twice to an unnamed specialist called **`der Krebs doctor`** (Canvas 533, `p. 588`).

On a deeply personal level, Canvas 456 (`p. 511`, left column) preserves Frater Bernardin's own pathography. Concluding a chapter on the magnetic transplantation of diseases (*transplantatio morborum*)—whereby gout (*Podagra*) can allegedly be transferred out of a human patient into a dog by feeding the animal warm milk boiled with nail pairings, hair, and poultices from the patient's inflamed joints—Bernardin anticipates the reader's objection: *"Now someone might say to me: why do you write so much about these cures when you yourself have the gout (*du selber hast das Podagra*)?"* He answers with two striking ethical and devotional arguments: first, that as a religious brother he accepts his chronic gout as a penitential cross sent by God in this life to purge his sins; and second, out of genuine compassion for animals, writing, *"Ah, I pity the poor beast, to which I would not wish to do such harm, inasmuch as a dog is already man's most loving and faithful creature"* (*"ach erbarmet mich das arme fich. welches ich ihme nit zu leidt thun möchte. dieweillen ein hund ohne des der Menschen liebs u[nd] thrau ist"*).

## Source-Critical Proof of Original Authorship vs. Copied First-Person Passages

Because early modern medical compilers occasionally copied first-person clinical claims out of printed exemplars, it is essential to verify that the *Frater Bernardin* passages on Canvases 450–456 represent original autobiographical composition by the scribe of MS.990 rather than a transcription of an earlier work. Four independent philological and paleographical proofs confirm that Frater Bernardin is the physical author-compiler of the codex, while also allowing us to distinguish his voice from two borrowed first-person passages elsewhere in the volume.

First, within Canvases 450–452 (`pp. 505–507`), there is a sharp linguistic boundary between the **impersonal theoretical excerpt** translated from a Latin iatrochemical textbook and Bernardin's **colloquial first-person Austrian-Bavarian narrative**. Under the rubricated heading *"Die Sympathetische Heilung ~"* (`p. 505`, right column, line 32 to `p. 507`, left column, line 18), the text adopts formal academic third-person prose dense with Latin terminology (*"in ein subiectum animale, oder Vegetabile... durch Unmittelbaren Contaktus... in Timæo Lib. 5. c. 8. p: 363... Spiritus Vitalis... Anima Mundi... Mumiam"*), and the first-person pronoun *ich* never appears once. Immediately after completing the definition of *Mumia* on `p. 507` (left column, line 18), Bernardin breaks off the textbook excerpt and resumes his personal voice on line 19: *"Widerumb mueß ich melden das ich selber erfahren von dem Effect dises Puluers. ich befande mich eines mall zu Grätz. und in dem alten Kloster in der Stadt..."*

Second, the manuscript preserves a unique **"double-telling" structure** between Canvas 450 (`p. 505`) and Canvases 454–455 (`pp. 509–510`). On `p. 505`, Bernardin initially recounts his dinner-table conversation at the castle of *"a very well-learned Baron whom for other reasons I do not wish to name by name"*, where he was addressed as *"mein lieber Frater Bernardin"* by *"a famous Jesuit theologian whom I also do not wish to set down here by name"*, followed by his private audience with *"a Bishop whom I also do not set down here by name"*. Four pages later (`p. 509`, Canvas 454), after reflecting on the physical length of his manuscript page (*"Von disen stuckhen Setze Ich mehr als einen Bogen zu überschreiben. Weillen es aber gar zu lang zu schreiben wierde also u[nd] laß ichs"*), he realizes that skeptical university physicians will reject anonymous witnesses. He therefore inserts a red `NB:` (`p. 509`, right column, line 23) and **re-narrates both episodes word-for-word with all the blanks filled in**: the unnamed Baron's castle becomes the estate of **Lord von Herberstein in July 1696**; the unnamed Jesuit becomes **Pater Höpel (*Regens in Convicto*)**; and the unnamed Bishop becomes **Prince-Bishop Franz Kaspar von Stadion of Lavant**, whom he attended for nine weeks alongside **Doctor Heibt**. No printed book or pre-existing literary exemplar would print two short anecdotes on `p. 505` declaring *"I refuse to name the Baron, the Jesuit, or the Bishop here"* and then re-tell the identical two stories four pages later solely to unmask their names.

Third, Canvas 455 (`p. 510`, left column, lines 6 and 18) exhibits **live authorial self-censorship in the manuscript ink**. At the exact points where Bernardin discloses Father Höpel's consultation with the Parisian theologian (line 6) and unmasks the Bishop of Lavant (line 18), the scribe writes a word, immediately crosses it out with horizontal ink strokes mid-line before proceeding, and reformulates the identifying phrase on the same line—demonstrating real-time composition rather than visual copying from an exemplar.

Fourth, a full-corpus scan of all 55,875 lines reveals that Frater Bernardin's editorial and clinical voice permeates the entire 741-page manuscript, while clearly separating his own statements from two borrowed first-person passages. Across the codex, Bernardin repeatedly cross-references his own alphabetical *Register* at the back of the book (e.g., Canvas 559 / `p. 614`: *"wie in registe Zu finden ist"*; Canvas 574 / `p. 629`: *"Von diser Kranckheit hab ich schon Vorhero geschriben, wie in Register Zu finden ist"*; Canvas 622 / `p. 683`: *"hab ich albereid guets geschriben wie im register Zu finden ist. alß khan der leser sich dort ersehn"*). He also inserts personal clinical observations across the medical and surgical books: noting on Canvas 9 (`p. 32`) the cures performed *in Wienn* with Balsam of Copaiba; recording on Canvas 179 (`p. 208`) how he drove away jaw swellings with elder-wood boiled in vinegar (*"hab ich mit holler hölzlein in + gesotten aufgelegt vnd vertriben"*); recommending on Canvas 181 (`p. 210`) barley mucilage *"for the poor"* (*"ist für die armen sehr gutt"*) and recounting how he softened parched febrile tongues with fresh butter and onion juice in rose-vinegar; framing his mistletoe secrets on Canvas 337 (`p. 367`) as revealed *"out of Christian love"* (*"auß Christlichen Lieb offenbahre"*); noting on Canvas 479 (`p. 534`) that he had happily cured many patients up to sixty years of age; remarking on Canvas 563 (`p. 618`) that he had personally questioned many learned men on whether mercury is hot or cold (*"habe ich bißhero Vill gelertten darüber verhört"*); and recording on Canvas 586 (`p. 641`) a bath with which he enabled rickety children to stand on their own legs. By contrast, only two passages in the manuscript contain a "borrowed first-person" copied from an older source: the cataract post-operative regimen on Canvases 30 (`p. 53`) and 47 (`p. 74`), where the *"ich"* belongs to Georg Bartisch's *Augen-Dienst* (1583/1686), and an alchemical provenance note for a Tincture of Antimony on Canvas 327 (`p. 355`), which recounts obtaining the process from the author's son and copying it out of a princely chamberlain's handbook.

## Visual Representation of the Graz Capuchin Infirmary, *Klosterapotheke*, and Alpine Fortifications

An examination of all **385 watercolor and ink illustrations** across MS.990 confirms that while the manuscript does not include literal topographical city views (*Veduten*) of exterior Graz landmarks such as the *Uhrturm*, its interior and background scenes provide an extraordinary visual record of **Frater Bernardin's own Capuchin apothecary (*Klosterapotheke*) and conventual infirmary in Graz**, together with one evocative fortified Inner Austrian landscape.

In the outdoor scenes, most figures stand on schematic grassy hillocks, with one notable exception on **Canvas 661 (`p. 725`)**, at the head of the treatise on head and battle wounds (*"Von Verwundtung des Haubts Vnd andern Thaillen des Leibs"*). Behind the wounded man seated on a stone block, the artist painted **steep blue-grey pre-Alpine mountains** rising behind a **massive cylindrical stone artillery bastion (*Kanonenbastei*)** with horizontal masonry courses and gun-embrasures, standing beside a steeply pitched gable-roofed building and a wayside cross. This juxtaposition of a heavy Renaissance round stone bastion against blue-grey foothills closely mirrors the fortified topography of seventeenth-century **Graz**, where the first Capuchin friary at the Paulustor sat directly beneath the massive Renaissance bastions of the **Graz Schloßberg** overlooking the Mur valley hills.

Even more significant are the recurring interior paintings of the **monastic pharmacy and infirmary** on **Canvas 322 (`p. 352`)**, **Canvas 381 (`p. 417`)**, and **Canvas 733 (`p. 542`)**, supplemented by the operative infirmary scenes on **Canvas 43 (`p. 70`)**, **Canvas 54 (`p. 83`)**, and **Canvas 59 (`p. 88`)**. A close comparison of Canvases 322 and 381 reveals that both full-page watercolors depict the **exact same monastic pharmacy room** from two slightly different angles:
1. Both rooms feature the same **terracotta-and-cream diamond-checkered tile floor** and thick whitewashed masonry walls pierced by a **four-pane leaded bull's-eye glass window (*Butzenscheibenfenster*)**.
2. Both rooms display the exact same **yellow wooden wall-cupboard (*Wandschrank*)** mounted on the whitewashed wall, fitted with two doors, **four black wrought-iron spear-head strap hinges**, and double keyhole escutcheons for locking away toxic *Venena* and opiates.
3. Both rooms show the same upper wooden shelving lined with **cylindrical painted wooden drug-boxes (*Holzbüchsen*)** in terracotta-red, yellow, and green with diagonal scroll labels, paired with **blue-and-white faience *albarelli*** and brick alchemical distillation furnaces on the tiled floor.
4. Across Canvases **43, 54, 59, 322, 381, and 733**, the artist repeatedly portrays the **same bearded, tonsured Capuchin lay brother in his brown pointed-hood habit and white knotted cord**—on Canvas 322 receiving a written prescription slip (`"℞..."`) across the worktable from a visiting physician or nobleman in a plumed hat, on Canvas 381 holding up a red tincture flask beside his brass mortar, on Canvas 733 dispensing an eye-water vial to a nobleman while blindfolded patients wait at the door, and on Canvases 43, 54, and 59 couching cataracts and operating on a veiled nun's lacrimal fistula. These interiors almost certainly represent eyewitness depictions of the Capuchin *Klosterapotheke* at the Paulustor in Graz, with the bearded Capuchin apothecary serving as a self-representation (or workshop portrait) of **Frater Bernardin** himself.

## Printed Precedents and Textual Sources Across the Manuscript

Rather than copying a single exemplar, Frater Bernardin constructed MS.990 as an encyclopedic clinical manual by synthesizing at least five distinct streams of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century medical literature, translating Latin terminology into Upper German (Bavarian-Austrian dialect) and pairing the text with 385 hand-colored ink and watercolor diagrams.

### 1. Ophthalmology (`pp. 48–174`, Canvases 25–145 & 727–733): Georg Bartisch's *Ophthalmodouleia* (1583 / 1686 Nuremberg Reissue) and the *Catoptrum Microcosmicum* Tradition

The 126-page ophthalmic treatise that forms the first illustrated book of MS.990 is a direct structural, clinical, and iconographic adaptation of **Georg Bartisch's (1535–1607) *Ophthalmodouleia, das ist Augendienst***, first printed in Dresden in 1583 and **reissued in a modernized quarto edition at Nuremberg in 1686** by Georg Scheurer and Abraham Lichtenthaler under the title *Augen-Dienst, Newer und wohlgegründeter Bericht von ursachen und erkäntnüs aller Gebrechen, Schäden und Mängel der Augen*. The timing of this **1686 Nuremberg edition** aligns remarkably with our newly established post-1696 dating of MS.990. Whereas Bartisch's 1583 Dresden folio featured Renaissance woodcuts in sixteenth-century Saxon attire, the 1686 Nuremberg edition re-engraved the plates in late-seventeenth-century dress and circulated widely in South German and Austrian apothecaries during the late 1680s and 1690s.

Frater Bernardin follows Bartisch's architecture step by step. He opens with the multi-layered anatomical structure of the eye (`p. 48`, Canvas 25, paired with the full-page layered flap-anatomy components preserved at the back of the codex on Canvases 727–730), enumerating the seven ocular tunics (*Coniunctiva, Cornea, Sclerotica, Uvea, Secundina, Aranea, Retina*) and the three humors (*Aqueus, Crystallinus, Vitreus*). For the Latin-German anatomical key on Canvas 25 (`p. 48`, *"A. Zaigt an das Tenuis membrana das markhicht so von dem Gehirn wechst. Substanzia medularis à Cerebri Substantza pronata..."*) and the moralized nomenclature of the extraocular muscles (`p. 49`, Canvas 26: *"das erste auffhebente hoffertige meüslein"* for *musculus superbus* / superior rectus; *"das erste Nider drükhente meüslein diemütigh"* for *musculus humilis* / inferior rectus), Bernardin supplements Bartisch with the seventeenth-century anatomical flap-broadsheet tradition of **Johann Remmelin's *Catoptrum Microcosmicum* (*Kleiner Welt-Spiegel*, Augsburg 1613/1619; German editions 1632/1661)** and Giulio Casseri's ocular plates.

The subsequent clinical chapters reproduce Bartisch's sequence and surgical instrumentation while adapting the human figures to a Catholic monastic milieu. The taxonomy of white, grey, blue, green, yellow, and black cataracts (*Star*, `pp. 53–68`), the preparation of the silver cataract needle (*Starnadel*), the seated couching procedure (`p. 69`, Canvas 41), and the post-operative green-cloth eye-shade (`p. 70`, Canvas 42) derive directly from Bartisch's Third through Fifth Parts. Most tellingly, when illustrating the surgical incision and cauterization of the lacrimal fistula (*Fistula lachrymalis*, `p. 83–84`, Canvases 54–55), the manuscript replaces Bartisch's secular Saxon barber-surgeon and burgher patient with a **brown-robed Franciscan/Capuchin friar operating on a veiled nun**, visually translating Protestant Electoral Saxon eye surgery into a Counter-Reformation Inner Austrian conventual infirmary. The later chapters on *Fell* (*pannus* and pterygium, `pp. 89–99`), hot and cold ocular fluxes (`pp. 99–122`), and the screw-clamped eyelid splint (*Klemm-Instrument*) for excising excess lid skin in trichiasis (`p. 129`, Canvas 100) likewise adapt Bartisch's Tenth and Eleventh Parts.

### 2. Internal Viscera, Complexions, and Diagnostics (`pp. 176–295`, Canvases 147–266): Vernacular Galenic Physiology and Monastic Phlebotomy

The second major division of the codex surveys the internal organs from the stomach, small and large intestines (*Duodenum, Iejunum, Ileum, Caecum, Colon, Rectum*), and omentum (`pp. 176–198`) through the liver, spleen, kidneys, urinary bladder, heart (`p. 255`, Canvas 228), lungs, and cerebral ventricles, followed by the four humoral temperaments (*Cholerisch, Sanguinisch, Melancholisch, Phlegmatisch*, `pp. 199–205`). Rather than following post-Harveian mechanical physiology, this section preserves classical Galenic-Avicennian pneumatology: the liver remains the seat of sanguification and *Spiritus Naturalis*, while the left ventricle of the heart generates the fiery *Spiritus Vitalis* (*Lebensgeister*) and innate heat (*angeborne Wärme*, `p. 255`). The anatomical watercolors of the excised stomach, intestinal coils, and heart chambers function as practical visual aids for friar-infirmarians who needed to correlate surface pain in the hypochondria or epigastrium with underlying visceral pathology.

### 3. Spagyric and Chymical Pharmacy (`pp. 350–465`, Canvases 321–425): Croll, Mynsicht, Ruland, Quercetanus, and Schröder

The core pharmaceutical books of MS.990 demonstrate that by the 1690s the Capuchin apothecary in Graz was thoroughly integrated into the post-Paracelsian **iatrochemical (spagyric)** mainstream. Frater Bernardin cites by name the foundational Lutheran, Calvinist, and Catholic chymical authorities of the seventeenth century, whose Latin pharmacopoeias circulated in massive German translations printed in Nuremberg and Frankfurt during the 1670s–1690s:

First, **Oswald Croll (c. 1560–1609)**, whose *Basilica Chymica* (Frankfurt, 1609; German translation *Chymisch Kleynod*, 1623/1647) is cited nine times across the manuscript, is the direct source for `Emplastrum Sticticum Crollii` (`p. 521` and `p. 674`), `Antimonium diaphoreticum`, `Specificum Anodynum`, and the preparation of `Arcanum Corallii` and `Magisterium Perlarum` (`pp. 394–401`). Second, **Adrian von Mynsicht (1603–1638)**, Count Palatine and body-physician to the Duke of Mecklenburg, whose *Thesaurus et Armamentarium Medico-Chymicum* (Hamburg, 1631; German translation *Medicinisch-Chymischer Schatz- und Rüst-Kammer*, Stuttgart/Nuremberg 1682, 1686, 1695) is cited ten times, supplies the recipe and therapeutic indications for `Emplastrum Diaphoreticum Mynsichti` (`p. 500`, `p. 677`, `p. 720`) and antimonial emetics (`Crocus metallorum`). Third, **Martin Ruland the Elder (1532–1602) and Younger (1569–1611)** are cited six times for `Balsamum Sulphuris Rulandi` (`p. 383`, Canvas 349, and indexed on Canvas 712), prepared by digesting flowers of sulfur in oil of nuts or turpentine. Fourth, the French royal physician **Joseph Du Chesne, Sieur de la Violette, called Quercetanus (c. 1544–1609)**, is cited for `Pilulae de Tartaro Quercetani` (`p. 789`, Canvas 709) and `Pilulae Marocostinae`.

The overarching organizational framework of Bernardin's chemical chapters—grouping preparations systematically into *Magisteria* (coral, pearls, tartar, scammony), *Flores* and *Sublimata* (benzoin, sulfur, antimony, sal ammoniac), *Feculae* (*Fecula Bryoniae*, *Fecula Aron*), *Tincturae*, *Elixiria* (*Elixir Proprietatis Paracelsi*), and metallic *Reguli* (*Regulus Antimonii Martialis*)—closely mirrors **Johann Schröder's (1600–1664) *Pharmacopoeia Medico-Chymica*** (Ulm, 1641), which became the standard encyclopedic textbook of German apothecaries through Friedrich Hoffmann's commentary and Georg Daniel Koschwitz's monumental German translation, ***Vollständige und nutzreiche Apotheke*** (Nuremberg, 1685 and 1693). Within this chemical corpus, Bernardin also incorporates famous medieval and early modern wonder-waters, most notably the pseudo-royal charter of **Queen Elisabeth of Hungary's Water** (*Aqua Reginae Hungariae*, Canvas 471, `p. 527`), transcribed in the first person (*"Ich Elisabetha Khünigin in Ungern 72 Jahr alt, am Podagra vnd Gicht..."*, "I, Elisabeth, Queen of Hungary, 72 years old, afflicted with gout and palsy...") and illustrated with a golden royal crown resting on a crimson cushion—a rosemary-spirit distillation that achieved peak popularity in Vienna and France during the 1670s and 1680s.

### 4. Sympathetic Medicine and Disease Transplantation (`pp. 505–511`, Canvases 450–456): Balthasar Timaeus von Güldenklee, Sir Kenelm Digby, and Jesuit Natural Philosophy

Perhaps the most intellectually revealing section of MS.990 is Frater Bernardin's seven-page treatise on sympathetic and magnetic healing (`pp. 505–511`), where he gives his only exact bibliographic page citation in the entire codex. On Canvas 451 (`p. 506`, right column, line 19), after describing the transplantation of gout (*Podagra*) into a wormwood bush (*Wermuth stock*) through the patient's urine and blood, alongside the magnetic use of *Persicaria* (smartweed/fleabane), Bernardin cites: **`"Timæo Lib. 5. c. 8. p: 363."`**

This citation refers to **Balthasar Timaeus von Güldenklee (1600–1667)**, chief court physician (*Archiatrus*) to Frederick William, the Great Elector of Brandenburg, and Mayor of Colberg, whose collected Latin case histories and consultations appeared in ***Casus Medicinales Praxi Triginta Sex Annorum Observati*** (Leipzig, 1662) and ***Opera Medico-Practica*** (Leipzig, 1667; repr. 1691). Timaeus documented clinical cases of sympathetic cures, magnetic wound treatments, and *transplantatio morborum* on page 363 of his *Epistolae et Consilia*, which were subsequently summarized in late-seventeenth-century iatrochemical compendia such as those of Michael Ettmüller and Johann Doläus.

For the preparation of the **Sympathetic Powder (*Pulvis Sympatheticus*)** on Canvas 453 (`p. 508`), Frater Bernardin prescribes taking Roman or Cyprian white vitriol (*Vitriolum album*), exposing it in a shallow dish to the hot rays of the sun when the Sun enters the zodiacal sign of **Leo in August**, stirring it daily until calcined to a snow-white powder by solar rays alone, and blending it with powdered gum tragacanth. This is the exact solar calcination receipt popularized by the English Catholic courtier and natural philosopher **Sir Kenelm Digby (1603–1665)** in his Montpellier discourse of 1658 (*Discours fait en une célèbre assemblée touchant la guérison des playes par la poudre de sympathie*), which was translated into German by Matthew Homborg in 1660 (*Eröffnung unterschiedlicher Heimlichkeiten der Natur... vornemlich von einem wunderbaren Geheimnüß in Heilung der Wunden, ohne Berührung, vermittelst deß Vitriols*) and went through over ten German editions by 1695, alongside the famous Latin anthology ***Theatrum Sympatheticum Auctum*** printed at Nuremberg by Johann Andreas Endter in 1662.

What makes Bernardin's account historically exceptional is his synthesis of Paracelsian vitalism with **Counter-Reformation Jesuit casuistry**. Drawing on the vocabulary of Paracelsus, Oswald Croll, and the Jesuit polymath **Athanasius Kircher, S.J. (1602–1680)** (*Magnes sive de Arte Magnetica*, 1641), Bernardin explains that all bodies share a subtle vital balsam (*Spiritus Vitalis* or *Mumie*) connected through the universal world-spirit (*Spiritus Mundi*). When blood or pus on a linen bandage is touched with solar-calcined vitriol, the solar rays carry the cooling balsamic virtue through the continuous aerial medium back to the open wound, where the heat of the inflammation attracts its kindred spirit magnetically. By grounding the phenomenon in corpuscular/spiritual radiation rather than incantations—and citing the endorsement of the Graz Jesuit Convict Regent Pater Höpel and a Parisian theology professor—Frater Bernardin legitimizes sympathetic wound therapy for Catholic monastic infirmaries.

### 5. Wound Surgery, Bone Fractures, and Limb Amputation (`pp. 521–780`, Canvases 466–704): Paracelsus, Felix Würtz, and Baroque Field Surgery

The final third of the manuscript comprises a comprehensive manual of external surgery (*Wundarznei*), covering gangrene and *Sphacelus* (`pp. 521–541`), syphilitic and mercurial salivation and sweating-box cures (*Schwitzkasten*, `p. 612`, Canvas 557), medicated bath cures (*Bad Chur*, `p. 622`, Canvas 567), tumors, scrofula, erysipelas (*Rotlauf*), breast cancer (`pp. 585–595`), gunshot and puncture wounds, compound fractures (`pp. 757–763`), and major limb amputation (`p. 765`, Canvas 699).

Throughout these chapters, Bernardin repeatedly invokes two sixteenth-century surgical authorities: **Paracelsus (1493–1541)** (*Grosse Wundartzney*, 1536), cited ten times for `Paracelsi Stich-Pflaster`, `Opodeldoc`, and mumial wound balsams; and the Basel master surgeon **Felix Würtz (c. 1514–c. 1574)**, whose classic vernacular treatise ***Practica der Wundartzney*** (Basel, 1563; reprinted sixteen times up to 1689) is cited four times (`Felix Wirzii Bruch-Pflaster`, `Würizi Salbe`, `p. 674`, `p. 759`). From Felix Würtz, Bernardin adopts his emphatic warning against applying greasy oils, hog's lard, or heavy fat-based salves to fresh fractures and erysipelatous limbs, noting with Würtz that fats block skin pores, trap acrid humors, and provoke putrid gangrene (*Brand*).

The treatise on bone fractures (`pp. 757–765`, Canvases 691–702) and limb amputation (`p. 765`, Canvas 699) also reflects the systematized military and hospital surgery of the late seventeenth century, closely paralleling works such as **Matthäus Gottfried Purmann's (1648–1711) *Rechter und wahrhaftiger Feldscher*** (1680) and ***Grosser und gantz neugewundener Lorbeer-Krantz oder Wund-Artzney*** (Frankfurt/Leipzig, 1692), as well as German editions of **Paul Barbette** and **Johannes Muys**. Bernardin divides fractures into four diagnostic classes: transverse simple fractures (*fractura*), comminuted or splintered fractures (*morpha* or *comminutio*), longitudinal hairline fissures (*fissura*), and compound fractures with protruding bone fragments. He details the two-bandage counter-winding technique soaked in warm vinegar-wine, the filing or sawing off of protruding bone spikes that cannot be reduced without tearing the periosteum, and an eight-formula surgical appendix (`pp. 761–764`) including `Das Rote Wund-Pflaster`, `Lac Virginis`, `Güldine Milch`, `Das Rote Unbekannte Pflaster`, `Das Graue Pflaster` (a calamine diapalma variant), `Felix Würtz's Bruch-Pflaster`, and `Das Sambungerische [Hamburgerische] süße Pflaster`.

Even in high-risk operative surgery, the text retains its Catholic monastic stamp. Opening the chapter on how to amputate a gangrenous arm or leg (*"Von abnemung der Verdorbenen arm Und fieß"*, Canvas 699, `p. 765`), Frater Bernardin instructs: *"When such a perilous case occurs, let one first of all have the patient confess and receive Holy Communion early in the morning beforehand; for without the help of the Supreme Physician nothing good can be accomplished"* (*"thu man erstlich patienten Vor hero in der früe beichten vn Comuciren lassen. dan ohne des obristen arzten hilff khan nichts güets auß gericht werden"*).

### 6. Charitable Domestic Medicine and *Dreckapotheke* (`pp. 781–790`, Canvases 705–710): The Poor Folks' Apothecary and Kristian Franz Paullini (1696)

The manuscript concludes on Canvases 705–709 (`pp. 781–789`) with a self-contained charitable tract titled **`Paurn, Vnd Armer Leüdt apotecken`** ("Peasants' and Poor Folks' Apothecary"), introduced by a three-line rhyming German proverb: *"Von Duökh Vnd schölz / Von öll Vnd schmalz / Von eier Vnd salz"* ("From dung and bark/peel, from oil and lard, from eggs and salt"). Addressed explicitly to rural peasants and indigent patients who could not afford costly East Indian spices or chemical magisteries from municipal apothecaries, this section compiles low-cost remedies made from kitchen staples (butter, linseed oil, eggs, salt, vinegar, soot) and an extensive zoological and excremental pharmacopoeia (`pp. 786–789`).

The animal and excremental remedies include burnt swallow powder for throat angina, earthworm oil for contracted sinews, frogspawn water (*Froschlaich*) for erysipelas, human skull scrapings (*Cranium humanum*) taken from an executed criminal (`"von einen gerichteten Menschen"`) for epilepsy, and the medicinal uses of goose, pigeon, hen, peacock, stag, hare, wolf, mouse, and cockroach dung. The inclusion of this tract at the very end of MS.990 (written in or after July 1696) coincides directly with the publication of the most famous German treatise on excremental and household medicine, **Kristian Franz Paullini's (1643–1712) *Heilsame Dreck-Apotheke, wie nemlich mit Koth und Urin fast alle, ja auch die schwerste, gifftigste Kranckheiten... glücklich curiret worden***, first published at Frankfurt am Main in **1696**, alongside late-seventeenth-century editions of **Michael Ettmüller's *Schröderus Dilucidatus*** and **Jeremias Liebe's *Armen-Apotheke***.

## Suggested Further Lines of Archival and Philological Enquiry

The internal evidence uncovered in MS.990 opens five concrete avenues for original archival and historical research that could form the basis of a peer-reviewed journal article or critical monograph on Counter-Reformation monastic medicine in Inner Austria.

### 1. Archival Identification of Frater Bernardin in the Styrian Capuchin Necrologies and Provincial Records

Because Capuchin friars were known solely by their religious name plus their place of birth (e.g., *Frater Bernardinus Graecensis* or *Frater Bernardinus Labacensis*), it should be possible to identify Frater Bernardin's secular surname, birth town, year of profession, and date of death in the archives of the **Styrian Capuchin Province (*Steirische Kapuzinerprovinz*)**. The province of Styria (which included the houses of Graz-Paulustor, Graz-Graben, Bruck an der Mur, Leibnitz, Radkersburg, Murau, Wolfsberg in Carinthia, and Ljubljana) was separated from the Venetian network in the early seventeenth century, and its historical necrologies (*Necrologium Provinciae Styriae OFMCap*), profession books, and conventual chronicles (*Annales*) are preserved today in the **Provinzarchiv der Kapuziner in Österreich und Südtirol (Innsbruck)** and the **Steiermärkisches Landesarchiv (Graz)**. Searching the necrology between **1696 and c. 1720** for a lay brother (*Laicus*) named **Frater Bernardinus** who served as infirmarian/apothecary (*Infirmarius* or *Apothecarius*) in the first Graz convent (*in antiquo conventu Graecensi*) and suffered from severe gout (*arthritide / podagra laborans*) promises an exact prosopographical match.

### 2. Tracing the July 1696 Herberstein Dinner and "Pater Högel/Höpel, Regens in Convicto" in Jesuit Catalogues

The annual personnel catalogues of the Austrian Province of the Society of Jesus (*Catalogi Breves* and *Catalogi Triennales Provinciae Austriae Societatis Iesu*), held in the **Archivum Romanum Societatis Iesu (ARSI, Rome)** and the **Austrian National Library / University of Graz Archives**, list every Jesuit priest and brother by college and office for each academic year. Consulting the **1695/1696** catalogue for the Jesuit Colleges of **Graz** and **Ljubljana (*Labacum*)** will identify the exact full name and biography of the *"Pater Höpel [or Högel], Regens in Convicto"* who dined with Frater Bernardin and Lord von Herberstein in July 1696. This will also resolve with certainty whether the handwriting on Canvas 454 (`p. 509`) reads *"Zu Labach auf seiner herschafft"* (Ljubljana/Carniola, where Sigismund Christoph von Herberstein was Prince-Bishop) or a Styrian Herberstein lordship near Graz (such as Lankowitz or Eggenberg).

### 3. The Medical Household of Prince-Bishop Franz Kaspar von Stadion of Lavant (r. 1673–1704)

Frater Bernardin's testimony on Canvas 455 (`p. 510`) that he spent nine weeks nursing **Prince-Bishop Franz Kaspar von Stadion of Lavant** in collaboration with **`docktor heibt`** invites an investigation of the episcopal records of the Diocese of Lavant, preserved in the **Archdiocesan Archives of Maribor (*Nadškofijski arhiv Maribor*)** and the **Carinthian Provincial Archives (*Kärntner Landesarchiv*, Klagenfurt)**. Episcopal household accounts, correspondence, or testamentary records from St. Andrä im Lavanttal between 1673 and 1696 may document the bishop's protracted nine-week illness, payments or alms to the Capuchin infirmarian (likely dispatched from the nearby Capuchin friary at **Wolfsberg in the Lavanttal**, founded in 1634 just ten kilometers from St. Andrä, or from Graz), and the full identity of the attending physician Doctor Heibt (*Heubt/Haupt*), as well as the regional physicians **`Doctor Hausenbach`** (`p. 708`) and **`der Krebs doctor`** (`p. 588`).

### 4. Visual Translation and the Monastic Artist-Apothecary Workshop

A dedicated art-historical comparison between the **385 watercolor and ink drawings** of MS.990 and the engraved plates of the **1686 Nuremberg edition of Georg Bartisch's *Augen-Dienst*** and **Johann Remmelin's *Catoptrum Microcosmicum*** would illuminate how printed anatomical and surgical imagery was copied, simplified, and confessionally adapted inside a monastic scriptorium. In particular, the physical mechanics of the surviving multi-layer anatomical flap-sheets (*Klappbilder*) bound at the rear of MS.990 (Canvases 727–730: the layered torso and the five concentric layers of the eye) warrant codicological study alongside Canvas 25 (`p. 48`) to determine whether those rear leaves were originally designed as cut-out paste-ins for the opening anatomical chapters or as large-format demonstration teaching sheets for training novice Capuchin infirmarians.

### 5. Provenance Trajectory from Styria (post-1780s Josephinist Dissolution) to Viennese Antiquarians (1922)

Finally, the physical journey of MS.990 between its compilation in Graz around 1696 and its purchase by the Wellcome Historical Medical Library in **1922** from the Viennese antiquarian firm **Gilhofer & Ranschburg** (recorded on Front Flyleaf 1r, Canvas 3, Accession No. `41913`) remains to be mapped. Many Styrian and Carinthian Capuchin libraries and apothecary archives were dispersed either during the **Josephinist monastery dissolutions (*Klosteraufhebungen*) of Emperor Joseph II in the 1780s**—when the first Graz Capuchin friary at the Paulustor was suppressed in **1786** and converted into a state mental asylum (*Irrenhaus*)—or during nineteenth-century institutional sales. Inspecting the missing opening leaves (`pp. 1–28`, lost prior to modern foliation), the watermark of the laid paper (visible on the macro conservation shots in Canvases 734–743), and Gilhofer & Ranschburg's 1921–1922 Viennese sales catalogues will establish how this remarkable Graz Capuchin surgical and pharmaceutical codex traveled from Styria to Vienna and London.
